a turn-based galaxy that moves once a day

Everyone plans in secret. The galaxy moves all at once. Then you wait.

Galaxies is a free, play-by-email space strategy game. Design a species, settle the stars, and submit your orders before the deadline. Turns resolve when everyone is in, or when the clock runs out.

See how a turn works

Slow on purpose. A game runs for in-game decades across real-life weeks. It is ad-supported and free, and it will stay that way.

plan, submit, wait

A turn is three moves, and then a deadline.

There is no real-time layer and nothing to click faster than anyone else. A turn stays open for a fixed window (a day is typical, and the host sets it when the game is created). You can open your orders as often as you like inside that window, and change your mind until it closes.

  1. Plan your orders

    Set production queues, move fleets, lay minefields, redirect research, open or close diplomacy. Nothing you do is visible to anyone else while you do it. You are working from your own intelligence file, which is only as good as what you have scanned.

  2. Submit before the deadline

    Submitting locks in your orders and tells the server you are ready. If every player submits early, the turn generates early. If someone goes quiet, the clock still runs out and the galaxy moves without them; their standing orders carry on, so a missed turn costs you tempo, not your empire.

  3. Read what the galaxy did

    Everyone's orders resolve together, in one pass, on the server. You get the new year back as a report: what your scanners picked up, what arrived in your systems, what happened in any battle you were part of, and what it cost. Then the next window opens.

Note

Turns are simultaneous, not alternating. Nobody moves first, and nobody waits their turn behind five other people. The order you submit in has no effect on the result.

more than it looks

Four systems doing most of the work.

The interface is quiet. What sits under it is a full 4X engine that has been played and argued over for years.

  • Design a species

    You do not pick a faction off a list. You spend points on primary and lesser racial traits, on the environment your people can survive, on growth rate, on how efficiently you research each of six technology fields, and on what your factories and mines cost. Those choices decide which planets are home to you and which are poison, for the rest of the game.

  • Real fog of war

    You see what your scanners and your ships have actually seen. The map you play on is your own intelligence file, built from scan reports, and it can be out of date or simply wrong. Planets you surveyed ten years ago still show ten-year-old numbers. Cloaked fleets may not show at all.

  • Simultaneous combat

    Battles resolve on the server during turn generation, round by round, from the battle orders and the ship designs you committed beforehand. You do not steer anything in the fight. You get the battle report afterward and find out whether your design, your stack sizes, and your initiative held up.

  • Victory on your terms

    The host sets the victory conditions when the game is created: planets held, technology levels reached, production, score, or a combination, plus how many years must pass before any of it counts. A game can be a race to a tech threshold or a long grind for territory, and you know which one you signed up for.

public or private

Join a public game, or bring your own table.

Public games sit in the lobby with their settings on the front: galaxy size, player count, victory conditions, and how long a turn stays open. You can read all of that before you commit to spending several weeks in it. Take an open seat and you are in at the next turn generation.

For a private game, create one and invite people by Gmail address. They get a mail with the link, sign in with the same Google account, and take their seat. Sign-in is Google only, which is a real limit: no Google account, no game.

Because it is asynchronous, time zones stop mattering. Nobody has to be awake at the same hour as anybody else. Your orders are due by a deadline, not at a scheduled session, so a table can be spread across three continents and still play at the same pace.

open AI contract

Bring your own brain.

A seat in a Galaxies game is defined by one open participant contract: read the turn file, return orders before the deadline. Whatever satisfies that contract can hold a seat.

That is the same door for all three kinds of player. The AIs that ship with the engine, written in C#, go through it. So does a community plug-in you wrote yourself and pointed at the same interface. So does an LLM-driven player that reads the turn report as text and writes orders back. None of them get privileged access, a private hook, or a look at anything a human player could not see; they all get the same intelligence file and the same deadline.

The contract is documented and versioned alongside the engine, so a bot you write today keeps working, and you can test it against the built-in AIs before you put it in a game with people in it.

built on Stars! Nova

Open, and credited to the people who did the work.

Galaxies is built on the Stars! Nova engine, an open-source (GPL v2) reimagining of the classic Stars!. The engine stays open, and the client source will be published from the same page as the download, with the build instructions and the commit hash it was built from, as GPL v2 requires. It is not published yet. That is a launch gate, not a preference.

Stars! itself was made by the original Stars! team, and it is the reason any of this exists. It was commercial software and its source was never released. Stars! Nova is a separate, independent, clean-room reimplementation of that design by its own volunteer contributors, written from scratch and licensed under GPL v2. Everything running under Galaxies is Stars! Nova's own code, carrying its own notices. Galaxies is not affiliated with, sponsored by, or endorsed by the authors or publishers of Stars!.

keep the lights on

The ads keep the servers on. Donations let me care less about the ads.

Galaxies is free and always will be. If it earns a place in your week, you can chip in. If not, play anyway.

Here is where the money goes: hosting the turn generator, storing every game's history, sending the deadline mail, and the hours it takes to keep all of it working. Donations are a straight outbound link to somewhere else. There is no subscription, no account to cancel, nothing on this site that takes a payment, and nothing behind a paywall, ever.

Ads appear on the marketing site, the lobby, profile pages, and the game-over summary. They never appear in the map, the orders screen, or a battle report. That is a permanent ad-free zone, and no amount of money changes it.
